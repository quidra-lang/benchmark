[Overview]
Write a line-oriented text editor similar to ed. It reads commands one line at a time from standard input, edits a sequence of lines held in memory in the "buffer", and writes the results to standard output. It handles addresses and ranges given by line numbers, the current line, the last line, relative positions, marks and string searches; appending, inserting and changing text; deleting, moving, copying, joining, indenting, converting letters between uppercase and lowercase, substituting strings, sorting; a cut buffer; undo and redo without a limit on the count; global commands; writing to and reading from named files in memory; and various kinds of display. For an invalid command it prints an error that gives the reason, and changes no state at all.

[Input format]
Input is read from standard input. Each line of input is a "command line", a "text line" or a "terminator line", and the first line is a command line.
- The line after a command line is normally the next command line. However, a "command line with text", as defined in [Processing] 2., is followed by zero or more text lines and then by exactly one terminator line. A terminator line is a line consisting of the single character "." (period), and the line after it is a command line again. In other words, the first line consisting of only "." after a command line with text is the terminator line, and the lines in between are text lines.
- A command line consisting of the single character "Q" is the last line of input. It marks the end of input and is not treated as a command. A text line that is "Q" is just text.
- A command line is 1 to 80 characters long and a text line is 1 to 60 characters long; both consist of printable ASCII characters (character codes 32 to 126), and their last character is not a space. The first character of a text line may be a space.
It is guaranteed that every line follows this format and the [Constraints]. However, the content of a command line is not necessarily valid (how that is handled is described in [Processing]).

[Processing]
Process the command lines one at a time, in input order.

1. State
The editor has the following state.
- Buffer: a sequence of zero or more lines. Initially empty. The lines of the buffer are numbered 1, 2, ... from the top, and these numbers are called line numbers. Below, "line k" means the line with line number k, and "$" means the number of lines in the buffer. Every line of the buffer consists of 1 to 80 printable ASCII characters, and its last character is not a space (the rules below guarantee that this always holds).
- Current line: an integer from 0 to $. Initially 0.
- Marks: for each of the 26 names, the lowercase letters a to z, the mark either points to one line of the buffer or points to nothing. Initially none of them points to anything.
- Cut buffer: a sequence of zero or more strings. Initially empty.
- Remembered pattern: a string or "none". Initially "none".
- Remembered replacement: a replacement string (13.) or "none". Initially "none".
- Display count: a positive integer. Initially 5.
- Undo history and redo history: sequences of "records" (15.). Both are initially empty.
- Files: a collection of named sequences of zero or more strings. Initially there are no files.

Besides its content, a line of the buffer has an identity, "which line it is". A mark points not to a line number but to the line itself; when the position of the line changes, the line number of the line the mark points to changes too. The commands treat line identity as follows.
- Every line that enters the buffer through a, i, c, t, x, r (read) or e is a new line (it is a different line even if its content equals that of an existing line).
- A line whose content is changed by s, >, < or ~, the first line that receives the result of joining in j and J, a line moved by m, and a line reordered by sort remain the same line as before.
- Lines deleted by d and c, the joined lines other than the first in j and J, and lines removed by the u option of sort leave the buffer.
- u and r (redo) return the buffer to a recorded state. In doing so, each line also becomes the same line it was when the state was recorded.
When the line a mark points to is not in the buffer, the mark is treated as pointing to nothing (if that line returns to the buffer, through u or otherwise, the mark points to that line again).

2. Structure of a command line
A command line consists of a leading "address part" and the remaining "command part". The address part is the sequence of the following "elements", cut out one after another from the start of the line, as many as possible. Each element is cut out as follows, depending on the character at that position.
  A digit: cut out all consecutive digits as a "number".
  "+" or "-": cut out that character together with all digits immediately following it (there may be zero of them) as an "offset".
  "." or "$": cut out that single character.
  "," or ";": cut out that single character as a "separator".
  "%": cut out that single character.
  "'": cut out that character and the one character immediately after it as a "mark reference", and that following character is the "mark name". If "'" is the last character of the line, cut out only "'", and there is no mark name.
  "/" or "?": read a "delimited string" (see below) with that character as the delimiter, and cut out the whole of it. "/" gives a "forward search" and "?" a "backward search", and the content read is the "search string".
  Any other character: the address part ends here (it also ends when the end of the line is reached).

A delimited string is read starting from the character after the delimiter. A pair of "\" and the character after it stands for that following character itself (the delimiter, "\" and "&" also become that character itself). The string ends where a delimiter appears that is not part of such a pair, and the part up to there (with the pairs replaced) is its content. If the line ends without a delimiter appearing (including the case where "\" is the last character of the line), it is the error missing delimiter. In a replacement string (13.), an "&" that is not part of a pair is treated specially. In all other situations "&" is an ordinary character.

The following two kinds of command lines are called "command lines with text".
- One for which cutting out the address part does not give missing delimiter and whose command part is a single character, "a", "i" or "c".
- One for which cutting out the address part does not give missing delimiter, whose command part starts with "g" or "v", whose command part has a second character that is neither a space nor "\", for which the delimited string read from the third character with that second character as the delimiter ends properly, and for which the remainder after it is a single character, "a", "i" or "c".
The sequence of text lines after a command line with text (not including the terminator line; it may have zero lines) is called the "text" of that command. Whether a command line has text is decided only by its form, as above, and does not depend on whether the command results in an error. The text lines and the terminator line of a command that results in an error are simply skipped.

3. Processing steps
Process each command line in the order (1) to (8) below.
(1) Cut out the address part (2.).
(2) Check the form of the address part (4.).
(3) Parse the command part (5.).
(4) Check the number of addresses (6.).
(5) Compute the values of the addresses (4.).
(6) Fill in omitted addresses and check the range (6.).
(7) Execute the command (8. to 17.).
(8) If the buffer has more than 200 lines, it is the error buffer full.
When an error occurs anywhere, stop processing at that point and print only the single line "? <reason>". <reason> is the name of the error, such as missing delimiter. In this case, restore the state (everything in 1.) to what it was before processing of that command line started, and do not print any lines that the command line would have printed up to that point either. When no error occurs, print the lines that the command prints, and if the command is a modifying command (15.), add a record.

4. Addresses
(Form) Interpret the sequence of elements of the address part as follows.
- If there are no elements, the number of addresses is 0.
- If the elements are exactly one "%", treat it as if "1,$" had been written.
- Otherwise, if there are two or more separators, it is bad address. If there is one separator, the sequence of elements before it is the "left" and the sequence after it is the "right" (either may be empty), and the number of addresses is 2. If there is no separator, the whole sequence of elements is one address, and the number of addresses is 1.
- A non-empty left, a non-empty right, and a single address must each have the form "one base followed by zero or more offsets" or "one or more offsets only". A base is a number, ".", "$", a mark reference, a forward search or a backward search. Otherwise (for example when a base is somewhere other than at the start, or when "%" is included) it is bad address.

(Value) The value of one address (a sequence of elements) is computed as follows.
- The value of the base is as follows. A number is its value (in decimal), "." is the current line, and "$" is the number of lines in the buffer. For a mark reference: if the mark name is not a lowercase letter (including when there is no mark name), it is invalid mark; if that mark does not point to a line, it is mark not set; otherwise it is the line number of that line. For a forward search and a backward search, determine the pattern from the search string (see below); a forward search examines the lines from the line after the current line to line $, then from line 1 to the current line, in increasing order of line number, and a backward search examines the lines from the line before the current line down to line 1, then from line $ down to the current line, in decreasing order of line number; the value is the line number of the first line that contains the pattern (the pattern appears as a contiguous part of the line's content). There is no line with line number 0, so it is not examined. If no line contains the pattern, it is no match.
- If there is no base, the current line is the value of the base.
- Add the value of each offset, in order, to the value of the base. The value of an offset starting with "+" is the value of the digits after it, and the value of an offset starting with "-" is the value of the digits after it multiplied by -1. If there are no digits, they are 1 and -1 respectively.
- At this stage, a value below 0 or greater than $ is not an error.
Determining the pattern: to determine the pattern from a string s, if s is not empty, make s the remembered pattern and use s as the pattern. If s is empty, use the remembered pattern as the pattern; if the remembered pattern is "none", it is no previous pattern.

If the number of addresses is 1, compute the value of that address. If the number of addresses is 2, compute the "left value" and the "right value" in the following order.
- First compute the left value. If the left is empty, it is 1 when the separator is "," and the current line when it is ";".
- If the separator is ";": if the left value is not between 0 and $ inclusive, it is address out of range. Otherwise, change the current line to the left value (this change remains as it is if the command does not result in an error).
- Then compute the right value. If the right is empty, it is $.

5. Parsing the command part
Parse the command part as follows to determine the kind of command and its arguments.
- If the command part is "sort" or starts with "sort " (sort and one space), it is the sort command (12.).
- Otherwise, the first character of the command part ("empty" if the command part is empty) determines the command, and the part from the second character on is called the "argument part". If the first character is none of the following, it is unknown command.
  empty, a, i, c, p, n, l, =, #, y, K, h, f: the argument part must be empty. If it is not empty, it is invalid suffix.
  d, x, j, J: the argument part must be empty or a single character, "p", "n" or "l" (a print suffix, 7.). Otherwise it is invalid suffix.
  z, >, <, u: the argument part must be empty or a number whose value is at least 1 (a sequence of one or more digits read as a decimal number). Otherwise it is invalid suffix.
  ~: the argument part must be empty, "u" or "l". Otherwise it is invalid suffix.
  r: if the argument part is empty or consists only of digits, it is "redo", and the argument part follows the same rule as for z (a value of 0 is invalid suffix). Otherwise it is "read", and the argument part must be a file name specification (see below).
  w: if the argument part is empty, it is "print". If it is not empty, it is "write", and the argument part must be a file name specification.
  W, e: the argument part must be a file name specification.
  k: the argument part must be exactly one lowercase letter (a mark name). Otherwise it is invalid mark.
  m, t: from the start of the argument part, cut out as many elements as possible in the same way as for the address part (this may give missing delimiter), and call that sequence of elements the "destination". If the destination is empty, contains a separator or "%", or does not have the form of a single address (4.), it is invalid destination. Otherwise, the remainder after the destination must be empty or a single print suffix character; otherwise it is invalid suffix.
  s: described in 13.
  g, v: described in 16.
A file name specification is one space followed by a file name, and a file name is a string of 1 to 8 characters consisting only of lowercase letters and digits. If the argument part is not a file name specification, it is invalid file name.

6. Number of addresses and ranges
The commands are divided into the following three kinds. In (4), the number of addresses is checked for each kind, and in (6), default values are filled in and the range is checked.
- Commands that take no address: u, r (redo), h, K, f, e. If the number of addresses is 1 or more, it is unexpected address.
- Commands that take one address: a, i, x, =, r (read), k, z. If the number of addresses is 2, it is too many addresses. If the number of addresses is 1, its value is "a"; if it is 0, the default value below is "a". If a is not within the range below, it is address out of range.
    a, i, x: the default value is the current line, and the range is 0 to $.
    =, r (read): the default value is $, and the range is 0 to $.
    k: the default value is the current line, and the range is 1 to $.
    z: the default value is the current line + 1, and the range is 1 to $.
- Commands that take a range: all the others (empty, p, n, l, #, w, W, c, d, y, j, J, m, t, s, >, <, ~, sort, g, v). A range is given by two values a and b, meaning line a through line b. If the number of addresses is 2, a is the left value and b is the right value; if it is 1, both a and b are its value; if it is 0, the following default values are used.
    g, v, sort: a=1, b=$.
    w, W, #: the whole buffer. Only in this case the check below is not performed (if the buffer is empty, the range has 0 lines).
    j, J: a=current line, b=current line+1.
    Otherwise: both a and b are the current line.
  If a and b are not each between 1 and $ inclusive, it is address out of range. Otherwise, if a>b, or if a=b for j or J, it is invalid range.

7. Display forms and print suffixes
When printing a line, one of the following three forms is used.
- p form: print the content of the line as it is.
- n form: print the concatenation, in this order, of the line number (padded on the left with spaces to 3 characters if it is shorter than 3 characters), one space, and the content of the line (example: " 12 text").
- l form: let S be the string obtained by replacing each "\" in the content of the line with "\\" (two "\"s) and appending "$" at the end. While the length of S exceeds 40, repeat: print one line consisting of the first 39 characters of S followed by "\", and remove those 39 characters from S. Finally, print the remaining S as one line. For example, if the content is "a\b", print "a\\b$".
For the commands d, x, j, J, m and t, when a print suffix is given, at the end of the command's execution, if the current line is 1 or more, print the current line in the form with the same name as the print suffix. The print suffix of s is described in 13.

8. Display commands
- empty (a command line consisting only of an address part): print line b in p form, and set the current line to b.
- p, n, l: print each line from line a to line b in order, in p form, n form and l form respectively, and set the current line to b.
- z: if the argument part has a number, change the display count to that value. Then print each line from line a to the smaller of line "a+display count-1" and line $, in order, in p form, and set the current line to the last line printed.
- =: print the value of a.
- #: for the lines of the range as a whole, print the number of lines, the number of words and the number of characters together on just one line, in the form "count lines=<lines> words=<words> chars=<chars>". The number of lines is the number of lines in the range, the number of words is the sum of the numbers of words in the lines of the range, and the number of characters is the sum of the lengths of the lines of the range. A word is a longest run of characters that contains no space.
- w (print): print each line of the range in order in p form, then print "lines=<lines> bytes=<bytes>". The number of bytes is the sum, over the lines of the range, of the length of the line plus 1.
=, # and w (print) do not change the current line.

9. Entering and deleting text
Below, k is the number of lines of the text (it may be 0).
- a: insert each line of the text, as new lines, in order, after line a (at the top if a=0). Set the current line to a+k.
- i: let p be the larger of a and 1, minus 1, and insert each line of the text, as new lines, in order, after line p (at the top if p=0). That is, if a>=1, insert before line a. Set the current line to p+k.
- c: replace the contents of the cut buffer with (a copy of) the contents of line a to line b. Then delete line a to line b, and insert each line of the text, as new lines, in order, at that position (after the original line a-1). If k>=1, set the current line to a+k-1; if k=0, determine the current line by the same rule as for d.
- d: replace the contents of the cut buffer with the contents of line a to line b, and delete line a to line b. If there is a line a after the deletion, set the current line to a; otherwise set it to $ after the deletion.

10. Cut buffer
- y: replace the contents of the cut buffer with the contents of line a to line b. The buffer and the current line do not change.
- x: if the cut buffer is empty, it is nothing to put. Otherwise, insert each line of the cut buffer, as new lines, in order, after line a (at the top if a=0), and set the current line to a+(the number of lines in the cut buffer). The cut buffer does not change.

11. Moving and copying
For m and t, after the range check, evaluate the destination as a single address by the rules of 4. (using the current line at that time). Call its value d. If d is not between 0 and $ inclusive, it is address out of range.
- m: if a<=d<b, it is invalid destination. Otherwise, remove line a to line b from the buffer, and insert the removed lines in their original order into the remaining sequence of lines: after the first d lines if d<a, or after the first d-(b-a+1) lines if d>=b. Set the current line to the new line number of the last of the moved lines.
- t: insert a copy (new lines) of line a to line b, in their original order, after line d (at the top if d=0). Set the current line to d+(b-a+1).

12. Line processing
- j, J: join line a to line b into one line. With j, the contents of those lines are concatenated in order as they are. With J, after removing all leading spaces from each line from line a+1 to line b, the content of line a and those lines are concatenated in order with one space between each pair. If the length of the concatenated result exceeds 80, it is line too long. Otherwise, change the content of line a to the concatenated result, delete line a+1 to line b, and set the current line to a.
- >: add w spaces at the start of each line from line a to line b. w is the number in the argument part, and is 4 when omitted. If any resulting line has a length exceeding 80, it is line too long.
- <: from each line from line a to line b, remove leading spaces, as many as the smaller of w and the number of consecutive spaces at the start. w is the same as for >.
- ~: for each line from line a to line b: if the argument part is empty, swap uppercase and lowercase letters; if it is "u", change lowercase letters to uppercase; if it is "l", change uppercase letters to lowercase. Characters other than letters do not change.
>, < and ~ set the current line to b.
- sort: the part of the command part after "sort " (empty if the command part is just "sort") is the "options". If the command part starts with "sort ", the options must be at least one character long, consist only of the characters r, u, i and n, contain each character at most once, and not contain both i and n. Otherwise it is invalid suffix. This check is performed in (3).
  On execution, sort the lines from line a to line b in ascending order of each line's "key". The key is the leading integer of the line if n is given, the content with uppercase letters changed to lowercase if i is given, and the content itself if neither is given. The leading integer of a line is, when the content starts with "-" followed by one or more digits, or with one or more digits, the value of the longest such part read as a decimal number (negative if there is a "-"), and 0 otherwise (examples: "-12ab" is -12, "007x" is 7, "-x" is 0). String keys are compared character by character from the start by character code, and if one is a prefix of the other, the shorter one is smaller.
  If r is given, sort in descending order. In both ascending and descending order, lines with equal keys keep their original order.
  If u is given, after sorting, in each run of consecutive lines with equal keys, keep only the first line and remove the rest from the buffer.
  Set the current line to the line number of the last line of the sorted range (a+the number of lines remaining in the range-1).

13. Substitution (s)
(Parsing) If the argument part is empty or consists only of "g", "p", "n", "l" and digits, it is a "repeated substitution". In this case, the search string is empty, the replacement string is "previous replacement", and the whole argument part is read as the flags. Otherwise, the first character of the argument part is the delimiter. If the delimiter is a space or "\", it is missing delimiter. Read a delimited string starting from the character after the delimiter as the "search string", then read a delimited string starting immediately after it as the "replacement string", and read the rest after that as the flags. However, if the original sequence of characters of the part read as the replacement string (between the second and third delimiters) is exactly the single character "%", the replacement string is "previous replacement".
The flags are read from the start, cutting out each time one of: the single character "g"; a single print suffix character ("p", "n" or "l"); or all consecutive digits (the count). If there is any other character, if any of g, the print suffix or the count appears two or more times, or if the value of the count is 0, it is invalid suffix. If the count is omitted, it is 1.
(Execution) After the range check, execute as follows.
- Determine the pattern from the search string (4.), and call it P.
- If the replacement string is "previous replacement", use the remembered replacement; if the remembered replacement is "none", it is no previous substitution. Otherwise use the replacement string. Make the replacement string used the remembered replacement (what is remembered is the string before the "&" replacement below, including, for each "&", whether it is part of a pair).
- Let R be the replacement string used, with each "&" that is not part of a pair replaced by P (an "&" that is part of a pair is "&" itself).
- Process each line from line a to line b in order. Search for the occurrences of P in the line's content from the start without overlap (after finding one occurrence, search next from immediately after that occurrence). Number the occurrences found 1, 2, ..., let N be the count, and replace with R only the N-th occurrence if g is not given, or all occurrences from the N-th on if g is given. A line with N or more occurrences is called a "substituted line"; a line with fewer than N occurrences is not changed. For the result of a substituted line: if its length exceeds 80, it is line too long; otherwise, if it is empty or its last character is a space, it is bad line.
- If there is no substituted line, it is no match (except when executed as the subcommand of a global command; see 16.). If there are substituted lines, set the current line to the last substituted line, and if a print suffix is given, print that line in the form with the same name as the print suffix.

14. Marks
- k: make the mark named by the mark name in the argument part point to line a (it no longer points to the line it pointed to before).
- K: print one line consisting of "marks" followed, for each mark that points to a line (excluding those whose line is not in the buffer), in alphabetical order of name, by one space and "<mark name>=<line number>". If there is no such mark, print "marks none".
k and K do not change the current line.

15. Undo and redo
The following commands are called "modifying commands": a, i, c, d, x, j, J, m, t, s, >, <, ~, sort, r (read), e, and g and v whose subcommand (16.) is something other than p, n or l. When a modifying command does not result in an error (even if the buffer content did not actually change), append a "record" consisting of the string of that command line and the buffer and current line from before processing of that command line started to the end of the undo history, and empty the redo history.
- u: let c be the number in the argument part (1 when omitted). If the undo history has fewer than c records, it is nothing to undo. Otherwise, repeat the following c times: remove the last record of the undo history, append a record consisting of the same command line string as that record and the current buffer and current line to the end of the redo history, and return the buffer and the current line to the state in the removed record.
- r (redo): do the same as u with the roles of the undo history and the redo history swapped. If the redo history has fewer than c records, it is nothing to redo.
- h: print "history undo=<number of undo history records> redo=<number of redo history records>", then print the records of the undo history from the last one backward in the form "undo <number>: <command line string>", and then the records of the redo history from the last one backward in the form "redo <number>: <command line string>", one line per record. The numbers each count from 1.
u, r (redo) and h do not add a record and do not empty the redo history. u and r restore only the buffer and the current line; the marks, cut buffer, remembered pattern, remembered replacement, display count and files do not change.

16. Global commands (g, v)
(Parsing) If the argument part is empty or its first character is a space or "\", it is missing delimiter. With the first character of the argument part as the delimiter, read a delimited string starting from the character after it as the "search string", and the rest after that is the "subcommand". If the subcommand is empty, it is taken to be "p". If the first character of the subcommand is none of p, n, l, d, m, t, j, J, s, >, <, ~, a, i, c, x, or if the subcommand has the form of the sort command (5.), it is invalid global command. Otherwise, parse the subcommand as a command part by the rules of 5. (any error that occurs there is reported as it is). The subcommand has no address part.
(Execution) After the range check, execute as follows.
- Determine the pattern from the search string (4.).
- Among the lines from line a to line b, select the lines that contain the pattern for g, or the lines that do not contain the pattern for v. If no line is selected, it is no match.
- Process the selected lines one at a time in their original order. If the line is in the buffer, set the current line to that line's line number, then execute the subcommand as a command with 0 addresses, including the default values and the range check of 6. If the subcommand is a, i or c, the text of the global command is used each time (the inserted lines are new lines each time). If a selected line has left the buffer because of an earlier subcommand, nothing is done for that line. A line moved by a subcommand is processed at its position after the move, and a line newly entered by a subcommand is not selected and so is not processed.
- When the subcommand is s, it is not an error if no substitution occurs on an individual line. If there is no substituted line in the whole global command, it is no match.
- When an error occurs while executing the subcommand, the whole global command results in that error.
The lines printed by the subcommands are printed in the order executed. No record is added per subcommand; if the global command is a modifying command, one record is added for the whole. The current line is the one determined by the last subcommand executed (if that subcommand did not change the current line, for example an s in which no substitution occurred, it stays at the line number set as the current line before that subcommand).

17. Files
- w (write): make the contents of the lines of the range the contents of the file with that file name (create the file if it does not exist, or replace its contents if it exists).
- W: append the contents of the lines of the range to the end of the file with that file name (create the file if it does not exist).
- r (read): if there is no file with that file name, it is no such file. If there is, insert each line of the file, as new lines, in order, after line a (at the top if a=0), and set the current line to a+(the number of lines of the file).
- e: if there is no file with that file name, it is no such file. If there is, replace the whole buffer with the lines of the file (new lines), and set the current line to the number of lines of the file.
These four commands print "file <file name> lines=<lines> bytes=<bytes>" at the end. The number of lines and the number of bytes are those of that file after the command is executed, and the number of bytes is the sum, over the lines of the file, of the length of the line plus 1. w (write) and W do not change the current line.
- f: print one line consisting of "files" followed, for all files arranged in lexicographic order of name (compared by character code, with the shorter one first if one is a prefix of the other), by one space and "<file name>=<lines>" for each. If there are no files, print "files none".
The contents of files are not restored by u and r (redo).

[Output format]
- For each command line, print only the lines described in [Processing]. Some command lines print nothing.
- Every number is written as a decimal integer without extra leading zeros (except for the spaces padded before the line number in n form).
- An output line starts with a space only when spaces are padded before the line number in n form, or when the string printed in p form or l form starts with a space (in l form, the second and later lines of a wrapped line may also start with a space). Trailing spaces never appear, and no empty lines are printed. Every line ends with a newline.
- The error lines are the following 23 kinds.
  ? missing delimiter
  ? bad address
  ? unknown command
  ? invalid suffix
  ? invalid mark
  ? invalid destination
  ? invalid global command
  ? invalid file name
  ? unexpected address
  ? too many addresses
  ? address out of range
  ? invalid range
  ? no match
  ? no previous pattern
  ? no previous substitution
  ? mark not set
  ? nothing to undo
  ? nothing to redo
  ? nothing to put
  ? no such file
  ? line too long
  ? bad line
  ? buffer full

[Constraints]
- The input has at most 2000 lines.
- The numbers in addresses and the digit parts of offsets, as well as the numbers in argument parts (the numbers of z, >, <, u and r and the count of s), are each sequences of at most 4 digits.
- When the n option is given to sort, the digit part of the leading integer of each line being sorted is at most 9 characters long.

[Example]
Input:
a
apple pie
banana split
cherry tart
.
2
s/an/AN/g
,n
1m$
kx
'x=
u
'x=
5p
g/rt/s/t/T/gp
w
h
Q

Output:
banana split
  1 apple pie
  2 bANANa split
  3 cherry tart
3
1
? address out of range
cherry TarT
apple pie
bANANa split
cherry TarT
lines=3 bytes=35
history undo=3 redo=0
undo 1: g/rt/s/t/T/gp
undo 2: s/an/AN/g
undo 3: a

In this example, "2" prints line 2 and sets the current line to 2, and "s/an/AN/g" replaces both occurrences of "an" in line 2. "1m$" moves line 1 to the end, and "kx" sets mark x on that line (line 3). When "u" undoes the move, the line mark x points to is back at line 1. "5p" is an error because there is no line 5, and changes nothing. The global command selects only line 3, which contains "rt", replaces every "t" with "T", and prints it in p form. Since the global command is a modifying command, the record that entered the redo history when "u" undid the move is discarded at this point.
