For the following maze of 9 rows and 13 columns, find the shortest path from the start to the goal using breadth-first search (BFS). In each cell of the maze, "#" is a wall, "." is a passage, "S" is the start and "G" is the goal.
#############
#S..#.......#
#.#.#.###.#.#
#.#...#...#.#
#.###.#.###.#
#...#...#...#
###.#####.#.#
#.......#..G#
#############
Each move goes one cell up, down, left or right to an adjacent cell, and wall cells cannot be entered. The S and G cells can also be passed through. The outer border of the maze consists entirely of walls.
In BFS, when a cell is reached for the first time, record which cell it was reached from (its parent cell), and reconstruct the path by following parent cells from the goal. In this data, the shortest path (the path with the fewest moves) is unique.

Output format:
On line 1, print the number of moves N of the shortest path in the format "steps: N", as a decimal integer without extra leading zeros.
Then print the 9 rows of the maze from top to bottom. However, for the cells on the shortest path other than S and G, replace "." with an ASCII asterisk "*". All other cells (S, G, walls and passages not on the shortest path) keep their original characters, and each line is exactly 13 characters long.
Put one ASCII space after the ":" of "steps:". Print only these 10 lines, with no trailing whitespace on any line, and end every line with a newline. The example output below is for a different maze of 5 rows and 7 columns (from top to bottom: "#######", "#S..#.#", "#.#...#", "#...#G#", "#######").

Example output (the numbers are illustrative and differ from the actual answer):
steps: 6
#######
#S**#.#
#.#***#
#...#G#
#######
