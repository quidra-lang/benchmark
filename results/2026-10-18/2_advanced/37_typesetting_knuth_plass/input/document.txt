.h1 Introduction {#intro}
Composition{idx:composition} is the craft of turning a stream of words
into lines, and lines into pages. A compositor working with metal type
made hundreds of small decisions every hour: where to end a line, how
much space to put between words, whether to divide a long word, and
where to start a new page. These notes describe how a program can make
the same decisions, and why some ways of making them give visibly better
results than others.

The examples in these notes are set in a monospace face, the kind of
type found on a typewriter or a terminal{idx:terminal}. Every character
occupies exactly one column, so the width of a line is simply the number
of characters in it. This removes the subtle questions of kerning and
letter fit, but it keeps every problem that matters for line
breaking{idx:line breaking}: words still have different lengths, and
spaces can still be stretched.
.footer ||{page}

A reader rarely notices good composition. What a reader notices is the
opposite: a line with huge gaps between its words, a column of holes
running down a justified paragraph, a heading stranded at the foot of a
page, or the last line of a paragraph left alone at the top of the next
one. Section {ref:greedy} shows how such defects arise, and section
{ref:total} explains how a better algorithm avoids most of
them{fn:The method was published by Knuth and Plass in 1981.}.

.h2 How the notes are organised
The notes follow the order in which a typesetting program works. Chapter
{ref:breaking} treats the breaking of a single paragraph into lines,
chapter {ref:hyphen} the division of words, and chapter {ref:pages} the
assembly of lines into pages with headers, footers and footnotes. The
notes borrow their vocabulary from the classic
manuals{cite:knuth84b,mittelbach}, and the index at the end of the notes
refers to every page on which a topic is discussed.

The classification of lines in table {ref:classes} on page
{page:classes} is the heart of the notes. Readers who are in a hurry may
go straight there and come back to the explanations later; the chapters
are written so that they can be read in any order.
.item Words are separated by spaces that may stretch but never shrink.
.item Lines may end at spaces, at explicit hyphens and at the
hyphenation points listed in a dictionary.
.item Every possible line is given a cost, and the costs of the lines of
a paragraph are added together.

.h1 Breaking paragraphs {#breaking}
.h2 The greedy method {#greedy}
The simplest way to break a paragraph is the one used by most word
processors{idx:word processor} and by every typist: fill the current
line with as many words as will fit, then start a new line and repeat.
This first-fit or greedy method{idx:greedy method} looks only at the
line it is working on. It never reconsiders a decision once the line has
been completed, and so it is fast, easy to understand and easy to
implement.

Its weakness is that a choice which looks harmless on one line can force
a bad choice a few lines later. Suppose the greedy method squeezes a
short word onto the end of a line. The next line then begins with that
word missing, which may leave it a few columns short, and the line after
that may be shorter still. The defect travels down the paragraph until
some lucky combination of word lengths absorbs it. A human compositor
would have noticed the trouble coming and taken the short word over to
the next line at once{idx:compositor}.

The greedy method can be improved by allowing it to divide words, and
that is what most programs do: when the next word does not fit, they
look for a hyphenation point that lets part of the word stay on the
current line. This fills the lines more evenly, but it also produces
many more divided words, and it does nothing about the basic
shortsightedness of the method{cite:kp81}.

.h2 Badness and cost {#badness}
To do better, a program needs a way to compare two ways of setting the
same paragraph. The usual approach gives every line a number called its
badness{idx:badness}, which grows as the line becomes looser. In these
notes the badness of a line is the square of its slack, where the slack
is the number of columns left over when the words of the line are set
with single spaces. A line with no slack has badness zero; a line with
three spare columns has badness nine; a line with six spare columns has
badness thirty-six. Squaring the slack makes one very loose line much
worse than two slightly loose ones, which matches what the eye sees. The
greedy method{idx:greedy method} never compares alternatives.

The badness alone does not describe everything a reader cares about. A
divided word costs something even if the line is perfectly tight, and
two in a row cost more than twice as much. Lines of very different
tightness next to each other look uneven, and a paragraph that ends with
a divided word on its second last line looks careless. Each of these
effects adds a fixed penalty to the cost of a line, and the cost of a
paragraph is the sum of the costs of its lines{idx:cost!of a paragraph}.
The values used for the examples are listed in table {ref:weights}.
.table lrl Penalties used in the examples {#weights}
Name | Value | Charged when
line | 10 | every line
hyphen | 50 | the line ends at a hyphenation point
double hyphen | 300 | the previous line also ended at one
final hyphen | 200 | the last line follows a divided word
fitness | 100 | neighbouring lines differ in tightness
.end

.h2 The total-fit method {#total}
Once every line has a cost, the best way of breaking a paragraph is
simply the one with the smallest total cost. The difficulty is that a
paragraph of a hundred words can be broken in millions of different
ways, far too many to try one after another. The idea behind the
total-fit method{idx:total-fit method}, described by Knuth and
Plass{cite:kp81,knuth84a}, is that the best way of setting the paragraph
up to a given break does not depend on what happens after that break.
The program can therefore walk through the possible breaks from left to
right and remember, for each of them, the cheapest way of reaching it.
When it arrives at the end of the paragraph, it follows the remembered
choices backwards and obtains the best set of
breaks{idx:dynamic programming}.

Two refinements make the method practical. First, a break can only be
reached from breaks that are close enough for the words between them to
fit on one line, so the program only has to look back over a small
window. Second, the cost of a line can depend on the line before it,
because of the penalties for consecutive divided words and for uneven
tightness; the program handles this by remembering the cheapest way of
reaching each break separately for each kind of line that can end there.
.quote Do not ask what the next line will look like. Ask what the whole
paragraph will look like, and let every line take its share of the
trouble.

The tightness of a line is measured by its fitness class; a penalty is
charged when two neighbouring lines are two or more classes apart. The
total-fit method never does worse than the greedy
method{idx:greedy method}, since it considers every set of breaks the
greedy method might choose, and on long paragraphs it is often better.
The gain is largest in narrow columns, where one long word can disturb
several
lines{fn:Narrow columns are common in newspapers, which is why newspaper typesetting was among the first applications of the method.}.
See table {ref:classes} on page {page:classes} for the classes.
.table clc Fitness classes of a line {#classes}
Class | Slack | Name
0 | 0 or 1 | tight
1 | 2 or 3 | decent
2 | 4 to 6 | loose
3 | 7 or more | very loose
.end

Not every paragraph is improved. When the words happen to fall well, the
greedy breaks are already the cheapest ones{idx:greedy method}, and both
methods produce the same lines; short items and quotations are often
such cases. The statistics file written next to the pages is a useful
reminder that the total-fit method is a refinement and not a miracle: it
can only choose among the breaks that the text makes
possible{idx:line breaking!limits of}.

.h1 Dividing words {#hyphen}
.h2 Where words may be divided
A word may be divided only at certain points, and the rules for finding
them differ from language to language. For English the safest source is
a dictionary{idx:dictionary} that lists the permitted points for each
word. A program can also use patterns that predict the points from the
letters of the word, a technique developed by Liang{cite:liang83}, but a
short list of exceptions is always needed for the words that the
patterns get wrong{idx:.hyphenation}{idx:hyphenation!exceptions}.
.hyphenation mono-space type-face hy-phen-ation

Besides the points from the dictionary, a line may also end after a
hyphen that is already part of the text, as in a well-known phrase or a
self-contained example. No extra hyphen is added in that case, but the
break still counts as a divided word for the penalties. Words with a
tie, such as Mr.~Knuth or page~7, are never broken at the tie: the tie
prints as an ordinary space but holds its two neighbours
together{idx:tie}. Some typesetters also refuse to divide the first two
or the last three letters of a word; these notes follow that custom, so
a word like {bad command} unity is never divided at all.
.term dictionary|A list of words in which the permitted division points
are marked with hyphens. It is consulted with the word in lower
case{idx:dictionary!lookup}.
.term pattern|A short sequence of letters with numbers between them; the
numbers vote for or against a division point.
.term exception list|Words that are always divided as listed, whatever
the patterns say{idx:hyphenation!exceptions}.

.h2 How much division is too much
Divided words are a cost{idx:cost}, not a goal. A paragraph in which
every other line ends with a hyphen is tiring to read, and the penalties
in table {ref:weights} are chosen so that the total-fit method divides a
word only when the alternative is a clearly looser line. The greedy
method has no such judgement: it divides whenever a piece fits, which is
why it usually produces more divided words than the total-fit method on
the same text{idx:hyphenation!frequency}.

The count of divided lines for each method is reported at the end of the
log, next to the number of pages. It is worth looking at both numbers
together: a method that saves a page by dividing many words has not
necessarily done
better{fn:Some publishers set an upper limit of three consecutive divided lines; the penalty for consecutive divisions in these notes makes even two in a row rare.}.

The paragraphs of this section are set ragged right, so the extra space
of each line collects at its right end instead of being spread between
the words. Ragged setting hides the defects of the greedy
method{idx:greedy method} quite well, because a loose line simply looks
a little shorter than its neighbours. The total-fit method still helps:
it keeps the right edge from swinging wildly between long and short
lines{idx:ragged right}.

Ragged setting is common for narrow columns, captions and code listings.
It is less common for books, where readers expect both edges of the text
to be straight.
.table lrl Effect of the penalties {#effect}
Method | Divided lines | Visible effect
greedy | many | ragged spacing and frequent divisions
total-fit | few | even spacing, divisions only where they pay
.end

.h1 Making pages {#pages}
.h2 Widows, orphans and headings {#widows}
Once the paragraphs have been broken into lines, the lines are poured
into pages. The simplest method again is greedy: fill each page with as
many lines as fit and start a new page. Here too a few rules prevent the
worst accidents. The first line of a paragraph should not be left alone
at the foot of a page, where it is called an orphan{idx:orphan}, and the
last line of a paragraph should not be carried alone to the top of the
next page, where it is called a widow{idx:widow}. A heading should never
be the last thing on a page; it belongs with the text it
introduces{idx:heading!keep with next}.

The page builder in these notes enforces these rules by allowing a page
to end only at certain places: between two blocks, provided the first is
not a heading, or inside a paragraph if enough lines stay on each side.
It ends each page at the last such place that still fits, which
sometimes leaves a few empty lines at the foot of a page. The log
reports a page as underfull when too many lines are left empty, so that
an editor can decide whether to rewrite a sentence{idx:underfull page}.

   | ........ |  orphan -> | x....... |
   | x....... |            | ........ |

.end

.h2 Footnotes, headers and numbers {#footnotes}
A footnote{idx:footnote} must appear on the same page as the place in
the text that refers to it. The page builder therefore counts the lines
of every footnote whose marker falls on the page, together with one
separator line, as part of the height of the page. If a line with a
marker does not fit together with its footnote{idx:footnote}, the line
moves to the next page and takes the footnote with
it{fn:Large typesetting systems may split a long footnote across two pages; these notes never do.}.

Headers and footers frame every page. The header of these notes shows
the current chapter on one side and the current section on the other,
and it is mirrored on even pages so that the chapter always appears on
the outer edge. The header is left out on the first page of a chapter,
where the chapter heading itself tells the reader where they are.
Chapters always begin on an odd page, so a blank page is inserted when
the previous chapter ends on an odd one{idx:blank page}.

The pages before the first chapter, the front matter{idx:front matter},
are numbered with small roman numerals, and the numbering starts again
at one with the first chapter. Cross references such as page
{page:intro} or page {page:widows}, the table of contents and the index
all use these printed numbers. A reference to a page that is not yet
known is printed as two question marks, and the whole document is set
again until every reference agrees with the pages it points
to{cite:lamport94}. A missing label, such as {ref:nowhere}, keeps its
question marks for good.
.item The table of contents lists every chapter and section with its
page.
.item The list of tables lists every table that has a caption.
.item The index lists every term with the pages that mention it,
followed by the entries below it and by any cross
references{idx:index!format}.
.item The references list every cited work, ordered by its
label{idx:references|bibliography}.

.h2 Points to remember
.enum Give every line a cost and minimise the sum over the paragraph.
.enum Prefer looser lines to divided words only up to a point.
.enum Charge extra for consecutive divided words and for a divided word
before the last line.
.enum Charge extra when neighbouring lines differ much in tightness.
.enum Never break at a tie, and never divide the first two or the last
three letters of a word.
.enum Keep headings with the text that follows them.
.enum Do not leave the first line of a paragraph alone at the foot of a
page.
.enum Do not carry the last line of a paragraph alone to the top of a
page.
.enum Keep every footnote on the page of its marker.
.enum Number the front matter with roman numerals.
.enum Set the document again until every page reference is stable.
.enum Read the log{idx:log

The last point deserves a final word. A long address such as
http://www.example.org/archive/typesetting/linebreaking/knuthplass1981/paper.html
cannot be divided anywhere, and a line that contains it is wider than
the column whatever the program does. The log reports such overfull
lines, together with justified lines that had to be stretched a great
deal, so that a person can rewrite the sentence. No algorithm replaces
that last check by a careful
reader{idx:composition!checking}{cite:bringhurst}.
