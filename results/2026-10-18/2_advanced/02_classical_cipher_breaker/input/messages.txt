MESSAGE lighthouse
The old lighthouse on the northern cape had not been lit for
many years, but every evening the keeper still climbed the
spiral stairs to polish the great lens. He liked to say that a
light which is cared for is never truly dark. Ships no longer
needed his beacon, since the harbor now relied on radio signals
and satellite charts, yet the village children loved to watch
him walk along the cliff path at sunset. One stormy night in
late autumn the power failed across the whole coast. A small
fishing boat was caught between the rocks and the open sea, and
its radio had fallen silent. The keeper lit the old lamp by
hand, turned the heavy brass crank, and kept the beam sweeping
over the water until dawn. The boat came home safely, and the
next spring the town council voted to keep the lighthouse
burning every night.
END

MESSAGE bread
Making good bread at home requires patience more than skill.
Begin with flour, water, salt and a little yeast, and mix them
only until no dry flour remains. Cover the bowl and let the
dough rest for half an hour, so that the flour can drink the
water and the gluten can begin to form on its own. Then fold the
dough over itself several times, wait again, and repeat the
folding until it feels smooth and springy. A long slow rise in a
cool kitchen develops a flavor that no quick recipe can match.
When the loaf has doubled in size, shape it gently, place it in
a very hot oven, and resist the temptation to open the door. A
crisp crust and an open crumb are the reward for waiting.
END

The mountain railway was built more than a century ago by
engineers who believed that no valley was too steep and no
winter too long. Its narrow track climbs through forty tunnels
and crosses twelve stone bridges before it reaches the high
pass. In summer the trains carry hikers, farmers and curious
travelers who press their faces against the windows to admire
the glaciers. In winter the line becomes a lifeline for the
remote villages, bringing food, medicine, mail and fuel when the
roads are buried under deep snow. The drivers know every curve
by heart, and they still sound the whistle twice when they pass
the small chapel at the summit, a custom that began on the day
the first train arrived.

the guide warned us that the next well lay three days to the
